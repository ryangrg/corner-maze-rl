1.Train vs. Val Loss

The first thing to look at in every run. Tells you whether the model is learning, whether it is overfitting, and whether the optimization itself is healthy — before you spend env time on inference.

1.1 What the loss is, in this pipeline

The Decision Transformer is trained to predict the next action given a window of (return-to-go, state, action) tokens. The training signal is a standard token-level cross-entropy loss over the 5-class action vocabulary {L, R, F, pickup, pause}.

loss = CE(â, a) averaged over every state position in the window (K positions per sample, BATCH_SIZE samples per step).

Each window contributes K predictions (the action at every step). The reported per-epoch train_loss / val_loss is the token-weighted mean — it is a per-step CE, not a per-window one. That matters: it makes losses comparable across runs with different K or different session lengths.

The two numbers you see every epoch come from the same loss function on different data:

QuantityComputed onGradient flows?What it tells you
train_loss The training split (windows the optimizer sees) Yes — this is what's being minimized Whether the optimizer is making progress at all
val_loss The held-out validation split (same rat, different sessions) No — pure inference Whether that progress generalizes to data not used for gradients
In this repo Train and val are split at the session level, not the window level. That means val windows are drawn from sessions the optimizer has never seen, which makes the val loss a genuine generalization signal (not a duplicate of train). Train/val differences here matter more than in setups that split by row.

Three CE values worth memorizing as anchors:

CE valueWhat it means
≈ 1.609= ln(5). Uniform random over 5 actions — the "knows nothing" baseline. Your first epoch should be close to this.
≈ 0.7 – 1.0Roughly the entropy of the marginal action distribution in the rat data (turns and forward dominate). Reaching this means the model has learned action frequencies but not yet context.
≲ 0.4The model is doing meaningfully context-dependent prediction. In our pose-visual runs this typically corresponds to ~80%+ token accuracy.

1.2 Anatomy of a healthy curve

Here is what a clean run looks like in this pipeline (drawn from a real CM016 acquisition run, idealized for clarity):

train loss val loss best-val epoch (where model_best.pt is saved)

Four phases, every one worth recognizing:

  1. ① Warmup — the LR is ramping linearly from 0 to LEARNING_RATE over WARMUP_STEPS (~5% of total steps in this repo). Loss may barely move, or may even tick up briefly. Not a problem unless it persists past the end of warmup.
  2. ② Rapid descent — once at full LR, both curves drop in lockstep. Train and val should track closely here. Big train↔val gaps already in this phase are a red flag (the model is memorizing fast).
  3. ③ Slow convergence — gains shrink, the gap between train and val widens a little, and the curves start to bend toward an asymptote. This is where most of the useful learning happens. Eyeball the slope: if it's still meaningfully negative, more epochs will help.
  4. ④ Plateau — train keeps inching down while val flattens or starts climbing. The model is now memorizing the train split. The EARLY_STOP_PATIENCE=20 counter starts counting val-no-improvement epochs and will eventually stop the run. Whatever model_best.pt was at the marker is what gets used downstream — not the final epoch.
Rule of thumb Healthy run: val loss is monotone-non-increasing through phase ③, train and val differ by less than ~10–20% of their absolute value, and the best-val epoch arrives well before N_EPOCHS. If best-val is the last epoch, you stopped too early — bump N_EPOCHS.

1.3 Six diagnostic patterns

Most pathological runs fall into one of these shapes. Each diagnostic below has a sketch plot and a recipe.

HealthyLockstep convergence

Both curves drop together, the gap is small and stable, and the run reaches a plateau before N_EPOCHS is exhausted.

Look for
Final val loss in a plausible range for this encoder (pose-visual: ≲ 0.5 is typical for CM016 acq); best-val epoch in the middle-to-late stretch, not at the end.
Do
Nothing. Move on to action accuracy and inference rollouts. Trust model_best.pt.

ManageableClassic overfitting

The model has run out of generalizable signal and is now memorizing training windows. The gap between train and val grows monotonically after the val minimum.

Look for
Train still descending while val plateaus or climbs; train/val ratio drops below ~0.7; patience counter eventually triggers early stop.
Do
Often nothing — the checkpointing system already grabs the best-val epoch. Worry only if val starts climbing very early. If it does: more dropout, smaller model, or more training data (e.g. pool more sessions).

BadUnderfitting / never learns

Train and val travel together but never make it below the marginal-action-entropy floor (~0.9). The model has not learned anything context-dependent.

Look for
Final val loss within a few percent of ln(5); near-zero gap between train and val; gradient norms shrinking toward zero.
Do
Diagnose in this order: (1) is LEARNING_RATE too low? (2) is the encoder lookup actually being indexed correctly — check that the state embeddings change between adjacent steps; (3) is the RTG flat across the window (e.g. degenerate cost config)? (4) is the dataset itself near-uniform — try image_cnn encoder for sanity.

BadDivergence / instability

The optimizer briefly works, then loss explodes. Almost always too-aggressive learning rate, a too-short warmup, or a corrupted batch (e.g. NaN inputs).

Look for
Sudden spike in train_loss; grad_norm jumps by 10× or more in the same epoch; first signs may be a single noisy val epoch before the train-side blow-up.
Do
Lower LEARNING_RATE by 2–5×, raise WARMUP_STEPS (≥ 10% of total), check for NaNs in the encoder lookup, and confirm gradient clipping is on (it is in this pipeline, but worth re-verifying if you modified Cell 6).

CaveatNoisy val (small holdout)

Common when the val split is small (per-rat runs with few sessions). The trend is correct but a single noisy epoch can falsely trigger or delay early stop, and model_best.pt can be captured at a lucky dip rather than the true minimum.

Look for
Train descends monotonically, val oscillates around a slow downward trend; epoch-to-epoch val changes much larger than the long-term drift.
Do
Trust the moving average, not the per-epoch value. Bump EARLY_STOP_PATIENCE so a single unlucky epoch doesn't kill the run. If you're training per-rat: pool sessions or pool subjects within the group to enlarge the val split. Always look at a multi-seed aggregate before drawing conclusions from one run.

SuspiciousVal systematically < train

The val loss should generally be ≥ train loss after warmup. If val is persistently lower, you are not measuring generalization — you are measuring an easier distribution.

Look for
Val loss line below train line for many consecutive epochs; final val loss notably lower than final train loss.
Do
Check whether the val split happens to consist of easier sessions (e.g. all the longest acq sessions, or sessions with overrepresented straight-runs). Also check for leakage: is the same session in both splits? Is dropout disabled in train (it shouldn't be)? With per-session splitting on small subjects this can also just be chance variance — confirm with multiple seeds.

1.4 A reading checklist

For each new run, work through these in order. Each one takes seconds and rules out a class of problems before you spend env time on rollouts.

  1. First-epoch sanity. Does train_loss start near ln(5) ≈ 1.61? If it starts much higher, you probably have bad initialization or a label-shift bug. If it starts much lower, you're either accidentally evaluating on a tiny easy subset, or your loss is being averaged wrongly.
  2. Warmup transition. By the end of WARMUP_STEPS, loss should be clearly trending down. If not, LR is too low or the encoder is producing degenerate (e.g. all-zero) embeddings.
  3. Shape match. Does the curve look like one of the six patterns above? Name it before reading the numbers.
  4. Best-val epoch position. If the best-val epoch is the final epoch, training was cut short — increase N_EPOCHS. If it's in the first ~10% of epochs, training is unstable or the model is severely overfitting from step 1.
  5. Train/val gap. A small, stable gap is healthy. A widening gap means overfitting (usually fine, the best-val checkpoint handles it). A negative or near-zero gap means data leakage or a too-easy val split.
  6. Across-seed consistency. A single run's curve is one realization. Before drawing any conclusion stronger than "the run didn't crash," compare best-val numbers across the N_RUNS seeds — variance across seeds tells you whether the shape is robust or a coincidence.
Where the numbers live Per-epoch losses are written to {seed_dir}/curves.parquet; per-batch losses (if SAVE_PER_BATCH_LOSS=True) go to per_batch_loss.parquet. Aggregated across seeds, training_aggregate.parquet reports best_val_loss_iqm/p25/p50/p75. Cell 12 plots these directly.

1.5 Common pitfalls in interpretation

  • Eyeballing absolute loss across runs. CE loss is comparable across runs only if the action distribution and the encoder are the same. A change of encoder shifts the achievable floor. A change of session mix shifts the marginal entropy. Comparing final_val_loss across these changes is meaningless — compare action accuracy or downstream env score instead.
  • Treating low loss as good behavior. Low loss means the model assigns high probability to the rat's actions. It does not mean the model would execute the right actions in the env. A DT can have val loss 0.3 and still fail rollouts because of RTG drift, off-manifold states, or stochastic action sampling. Loss is the first filter, not the verdict.
  • Confusing loss curves with learning curves. "Train loss" is per token; the learning curve (Section 3, planned) is per session or per trial in the env. They can disagree: training loss can keep dropping while env score stagnates if the model is over-fitting fine-grained action distribution at the expense of long-horizon strategy.
  • Reading a single seed. Per-rat per-seed runs are noisy on small subjects. A val curve that "looks bad" on seed 0 may be normal across seeds 0–4. Always plot the seed band, not just the median.
  • Forgetting the warmup. The first few epochs are LR-limited, not capacity-limited. Don't conclude from epoch 1–2 alone that "the model isn't learning."
